AI in Cinema: What Ben Affleck's Netflix Deal Means for Hollywood and Bollywood

Ben Affleck spent four years running an AI company that almost nobody knew about. In March 2026, Netflix bought it. The company is called InterPositive, and the deal has become one of the clearest signs of where film-making is heading, in Hollywood and in Mumbai alike.

What InterPositive Actually Does

This is not the "type a sentence, get a movie" kind of AI. According to Variety, InterPositive builds a custom AI model from a production's own dailies, the raw footage shot each day. Filmmakers then use it in post-production for things like relighting a scene, colour and sound mixing and visual effects. Affleck said it is "not about text-prompting or generating something from nothing."

Other reports describe uses such as swapping backgrounds, removing visible safety rigging from stunts and recovering shots that were missed on set. In short, it is a finishing tool for footage that already exists, not a replacement for the cast and crew.

The Deal in Brief

Netflix has not disclosed the price, though AFP reported a figure of US$587 million in July. All 16 members of the InterPositive team moved to Netflix, and Affleck became a senior adviser. Netflix has said it has no plans to sell the technology, and will offer it to its own creative partners instead.

Netflix's co-CEO Ted Sarandos said in July that generative AI workflows have been used on roughly 300 Netflix titles, mostly in post-production, according to AFP.

Why Hollywood Is Watching

The deal arrived as Hollywood's unions prepared for new contract talks, and AI was a central issue in the 2023 strikes. Online, there was backlash that Affleck's ownership had stayed quiet for so long. AFP reports that other studios are taking different routes: Lionsgate took a stake in Runway, Google invested in A24, and the traditional majors have officially stayed out so far.

And Bollywood?

India is already living the same debate. When Raanjhanaa returned to cinemas on 1 August 2025 with an AI-generated alternate ending, the director publicly objected, and legal commentators noted that Indian law gives the producer, not the director, authorship of a film. Indian courts have also been asked to protect stars from AI imitation. The Bombay High Court sided with singer Arijit Singh in 2024 after his voice was cloned, and the Delhi High Court gave actor Arjun Kapoor interim protection in April 2026, according to one report.

The pattern is the same on both sides of the world: studios want cheaper, faster finishing, while actors, writers and directors want consent, credit and control.

What It Means for Fans

For viewers, the near-term change is mostly invisible. Cleaner VFX, fewer reshoots and quicker turnaround rarely show up on screen, but they decide which films get made at all. If a tool like this lowers costs, it could mean more mid-budget films. If it is used to rewrite or imitate real performers without permission, expect more court cases and more public arguments.
